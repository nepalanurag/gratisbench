Vector tracing turns pixel images into infinitely scalable SVG paths: the logo that looks crisp on a business card and on a billboard. The best-known tool for the job, Vector Magic, traces for free but puts the actual SVG download behind a $9.95-a-month paywall. The paywall is not on the work, it is on the file you already made.

This tracer runs entirely in your browser and the SVG download is free. Drop in a logo, icon, or high-contrast illustration, choose color or monochrome, set the detail level, and download real vector paths you can open in Illustrator, Figma, Inkscape, or any browser. A PNG preview of the trace is included so you can check the result before downloading.

An honest note on what tracing can and cannot do: it excels at flat, high-contrast art with clean edges. It does not turn photographs into good vectors; a photo traced at high detail becomes thousands of tiny paths that look worse than the original and weigh more. If your source is a photo, you want a different tool. If it is a logo, you are in the right place.

How to use it

  1. Drop a PNG or JPG onto the box above, or click it to browse.
  2. Choose color tracing for multi-color art or monochrome for single-color logos and icons.
  3. Set the detail slider. Low gives smooth, simple shapes; high keeps fine detail with more paths.
  4. Click "Trace image". The trace runs on your device, usually in a few seconds.
  5. Preview the vector result, then download the SVG free, or grab a PNG render of it.

Common questions

Is the SVG download really free?
Yes. Vector Magic charges $9.95 a month for the download, but tracing is pure computation with no server cost here: it runs on your device, so the SVG is free with no account, no watermark, and no daily limit. The file is standard SVG that opens in Illustrator, Figma, Inkscape, and browsers.
Are my images uploaded anywhere?
No. The tracing engine is a small script that ships with this page, and the trace runs on your device. Nothing is uploaded, which also means this works for client logos and unreleased designs.
What traces well and what does not?
Logos, icons, pictograms, line art, and high-contrast illustrations trace beautifully: clean edges become clean paths. Photographs do not trace well; at high detail they become thousands of tiny paths that look muddy and weigh megabytes. Gradients turn into banded blobs of flat color. Match the tool to flat art.
What does the detail slider change?
In color mode it sets the palette size (2 to 16 colors) and how aggressively tiny noise paths are discarded; in monochrome it controls the noise filtering. Low detail gives smooth, simple, small files. High detail keeps fine texture but produces more paths and larger files. Start in the middle and adjust.
Why does my traced photo look bad?
Because tracing is the wrong tool for photos. A tracer sees only flat regions of similar color, so skin tones, shadows, and textures become posterized blobs. For photos you want the original pixels; for logos and icons you want vectors. No setting fixes a photo source.
My transparent PNG traced with a white background. Why?
The tracer works on opaque pixels, so transparent regions are filled with white before tracing. If you need the transparency preserved as a cut-out, trace the opaque version and delete the white background path in a vector editor, or use the background remover first and trace the result.
How large can the input image be?
Any size, but the long edge is scaled to 1024 px before tracing to keep it fast. That sounds like a downgrade, but tracing is about shapes, not pixels: the SVG output scales infinitely regardless of the trace resolution, and 1024 px captures all the edge detail a logo needs.
Can I edit the SVG afterwards?
Yes. It is a standard SVG with one path per color region, so any vector editor can open it: ungroup, recolor, simplify paths, or combine shapes. The paths use rounded coordinates, which keeps files small without visible quality loss.
