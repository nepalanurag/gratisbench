Optical character recognition is one of those features software companies love to gate: Adobe keeps decent OCR behind an Acrobat Pro subscription, and most free online OCR tools cap you at a few pages or hold the download hostage. Yet the underlying technology is decades old and runs fine on your own hardware.

This tool runs the Tesseract OCR engine directly in your browser. Drop in a photo of a document, a screenshot, or a scan, and it reads the text into an editable box you can copy or download as a .txt file. Before recognition it upscales small images and stretches contrast on a grayscale copy, the two preprocessing steps that genuinely move the needle on accuracy. Your image never leaves your device.

Be honest with yourself about the input: OCR is only as good as the pixels. Clean, high-contrast, straight-on text at a decent size reads near-perfectly. Blurry photos, decorative fonts, handwriting, and text over busy backgrounds will produce errors you need to proofread. The tool tells you this up front because a confident wrong transcript is worse than no transcript.

How to use it

  1. Drop an image onto the box above, or click it to browse. PNG, JPG, and WebP all work.
  2. Pick the language of the text. English is the default; eleven more languages are available.
  3. Leave the preprocessing options on unless you have a reason not to: upscaling and contrast help most images.
  4. Click "Read text". The first run downloads the OCR engine and language data (about 15 MB, once per browser).
  5. Copy the text or download it as a .txt file. Proofread anything important: OCR can misread blurry or stylized text.

Common questions

Is this OCR tool really free?
Yes. Adobe and others put OCR behind paid plans because they run it on their servers. Tesseract is a mature open-source engine, and this page runs it on your own device, so there is nothing to charge for: no page caps, no account, no watermark on the text.
Are my images uploaded to a server?
No. The OCR engine and language data download from a CDN once, but your image is processed entirely in your browser. That makes this safe for documents you would not want to upload, like receipts, IDs, or private correspondence.
What kind of images give the best results?
Clean, high-contrast, straight-on text at a decent size: scans, screenshots of documents, well-lit photos of printed pages. Blurry photos, skewed angles, decorative or script fonts, and text over busy backgrounds all hurt accuracy. If the result is poor, try a sharper photo before blaming the tool.
Can it read handwriting?
Not reliably. Tesseract is built for printed text. Neat block handwriting sometimes produces partial results, but cursive and messy writing will mostly come back as errors. Treat any handwriting output as a rough draft at best.
Which languages are supported?
Twelve: English, French, German, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, Dutch, Russian, Japanese, Chinese (Simplified), Hindi, and Arabic. Pick the matching language before running; the wrong language data is the most common cause of gibberish output.
What do the preprocessing options do?
Upscaling enlarges small images, because Tesseract expects text at roughly 300 DPI and tiny text is its biggest weakness. The contrast option converts to grayscale and stretches the tonal range, which helps faded scans and uneven lighting. Both are on by default and help far more often than they hurt.
Why is some text missing or wrong?
Usually the input: text smaller than about 10 pixels tall, low contrast, glare, or heavy compression artifacts. Tables and multi-column layouts can also come out in the wrong reading order. Re-shoot or re-scan at higher quality, make sure the page fills the frame, and proofread the result.
How accurate is it compared to Adobe Acrobat OCR?
On clean printed text the results are comparable: both get the easy cases right. Acrobat has an edge on difficult layouts, skewed scans with auto-deskew, and handwriting-adjacent fonts. For straightforward documents, this free in-browser version is more than enough.
