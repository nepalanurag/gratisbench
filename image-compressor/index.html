    Image files are heavy because cameras and screenshots err on the side of too much data: a phone photo can be 5 MB when 800 KB would look identical on screen. Most online compressors solve this by uploading your photos to a server, queuing them, and making you wait. This one re-encodes each image right here in your browser, so a batch of twenty photos compresses in seconds with nobody else ever seeing them.

    The honest part first: compression is a trade-off, and the trade-off depends on the format. JPEG and WebP are lossy, which means lower quality settings genuinely throw away detail to save space. PNG is lossless, which means re-encoding a PNG as a PNG barely changes its size at all. If you feed this tool a PNG and pick "keep original", expect almost no savings; switch the output to JPEG or WebP and the file will shrink properly.

    You stay in control of the whole process. The quality slider sets how aggressive the compression is, the format choice decides whether you keep the original type or convert, and every file shows its original and compressed size side by side with the percentage saved. If a result looks too soft, move the slider up and run it again. Nothing is final until you download.

    How to use it

    1. Drop your images onto the box above, or click it to browse. PNG, JPEG, WebP, GIF, BMP, and TIFF are accepted.
    2. Set the quality slider. 80 is a good balance for photos; go lower for smaller files, higher when detail matters.
    3. Pick an output format: keep the original, or convert to JPEG or WebP. Remember that PNG stays nearly the same size unless you convert it.
    4. Click "Compress images". Each file shows its original size, compressed size, and the percentage saved.
    5. Download files individually, or take them all at once with "Download all as ZIP".

    Common questions

    How much smaller will my images get?
    It depends on the input. A JPEG photo at quality 80 typically shrinks 40 to 70 percent compared to a camera original, with no visible difference at normal viewing sizes. Screenshots with flat colors compress less as JPEG but do well as WebP. PNG files barely shrink at all unless you convert them to JPEG or WebP, because PNG is already lossless. The tool shows the exact saving per file, so you will know immediately.
    Why did my PNG barely get smaller?
    Because PNG is a lossless format: it already stores your image as compactly as it can without losing data. Re-encoding it as PNG just rearranges the same information. Real savings come from switching to a lossy format. Convert that PNG to WebP at quality 80 and it will usually drop to a fraction of its size while still looking sharp.
    Does compressing reduce image quality?
    For JPEG and WebP output, yes, in principle: those formats discard fine detail to save space, and the lower the quality setting, the more they discard. In practice, quality 80 looks identical to the original for most photos. PNG output loses nothing at all. If a result looks too soft, raise the slider and compress again. Nothing is final until you download.
    What does the quality slider actually change?
    It sets the encoder quality from 5 (tiny files, visibly rough) to 100 (near-original, barely smaller). The setting applies to JPEG and WebP output; PNG ignores it because PNG has no quality setting. A good rule of thumb: 80 for photos going on the web, 60 to 70 when file size matters more than fine detail, and 90 or above for images you plan to edit further.
    Are my images uploaded anywhere?
    No. The compression runs entirely in your browser using code that ships with this page. Your photos never leave your device, which is also why there are no queues and no waiting. Closing the tab removes every trace.
    What happens to transparency in my PNGs?
    PNG and WebP outputs keep the alpha channel, so transparent areas stay transparent. JPEG has no concept of transparency, so converting a transparent image to JPEG needs a background color. The tool warns you when this applies and offers a white fill; without it, transparent areas turn black. The warning only appears when the image actually has transparent pixels.
    Can I compress many images at once?
    Yes. Drop a whole batch, set the quality and format once, and compress everything in one run. Each file gets its own before/after sizes and download button, and "Download all as ZIP" packages the batch into a single file. There is no cap on the number of images beyond what your device can comfortably hold in memory.
